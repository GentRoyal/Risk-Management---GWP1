# GWP1 Problem Formulation

## 1. The Problem

Alvi's dissertation addresses the problem of forecasting the spot price of crude oil using probabilistic graphical models (PGMs) (Alvi, abstract; sec. 1.2). The work is motivated by oil's role in the global economy. Crude oil is used both in conventional fuels and in building infrastructure, so it can heavily influence the economic growth of countries, especially oil importers (Alvi, sec. 1.1). Unexpected fluctuations in the real price of oil harm both oil-importing and oil-exporting economies. Reliable forecasts are of interest to global macro hedge funds, which treat the oil price as a key input to macroeconomic projections, and to policymakers, who use it to anticipate recessions (Alvi, sec. 1.1). Alvi frames oil trading as a global macro strategy, in which macroeconomic indicators serve as buy and sell signals on a monthly or quarterly scale (Alvi, sec. 3.1).

Within this setting, Alvi identifies three specific problems (Alvi, sec. 1.1). First, existing models of the oil spot price, such as GARCH, assume the error variance follows an ARMA process and do not incorporate the macroeconomic, microeconomic and geopolitical events that drive the price. Second, the Energy Information Administration (EIA) publishes so many datasets that relating them through expert knowledge alone is slow and error-prone, and any such model must keep changing as market dynamics change. Alvi therefore proposes learning the network from data, causally relating the datasets without an expert. Third, he raises the question of how the futures price of crude oil relates to forecasts of the spot price, since futures reflect buyer sentiment about future delivery.

The dissertation's hypothesis is that PGMs, combined with machine learning and statistical techniques, can improve the accuracy of existing quantitative models of the oil spot price by taking many macroeconomic and geopolitical variables into account (Alvi, sec. 1.2). To test it, Alvi sets four tasks: understand the macroeconomic structure of the energy markets; extract relevant datasets from U.S. government open-data sources, namely the EIA and the Federal Reserve Economic Data (FRED) service; construct a Bayesian belief network from those datasets; and test the model through historical backtesting, including stress testing (Alvi, secs. 1.2.1–1.2.4, 1.3). In the implementation, the target is the regime of next month's West Texas Intermediate (WTI) spot price, classified as bull, bear or stagnant (Alvi, secs. 4.1.3, 4.2, 4.3).

## 2. Why Bayesian Networks Suit the Problem

The oil price is shaped by many interacting factors. The EIA lists non-OPEC and OPEC production, non-OECD and OECD consumption, OECD inventories, proven reserves, geopolitical events and trading in financial markets as the main drivers (Alvi, sec. 3.1.1). Macroeconomic indicators such as GDP growth, the Consumer Price Index and the Industrial Production Index also shape the direction of energy markets (Alvi, sec. 1.2.1). Alvi argues that belief networks are highly applicable to capturing causal relationships among both data-driven economic variables and quantified expert judgements about oil-market geopolitics, in particular OPEC members' production and capacity policy (Alvi, sec. 3.4). It is a directed acyclic graph in which each node is a random variable and each edge is a direct dependence, and it is used primarily to represent causal relationships between variables (Alvi, sec. 2.1.1). The joint distribution factorizes into one conditional distribution per variable given its parents:

```latex
p(x_1, \dots, x_D) = \prod_{i=1}^{D} p(x_i \mid pa(x_i))
```

Alvi notes that giving a probabilistic model a graphical structure makes it both better specified and more computationally efficient (Alvi, sec. 2.1).

To address the second problem, Alvi proposes learning the network's structure from data, because building it from expert knowledge alone is infeasible given the growing volume of data and the changing structure of commodity markets (Alvi, sec. 3.4). Alvi uses score-based structure learning with the Hill Climbing algorithm (Alvi, sec. 3.4). He notes that Hill Climbing started without any initial structure can produce causally incorrect networks and a drastically different network each time it is re-learned during validation, since it can settle at different local maxima. He therefore seeds the search with an expert-drafted network based on the laws of supply and demand: its six edges have the EIA series STEO.PAPR\_NONOPEC.M, STEO.PAPR\_OPEC.M, STEO.PATC\_OECD.M and STEO.PATC\_NON\_OECD.M each pointing to the WTI price, STEO.RGDPQ\_OECD.M pointing to STEO.PATC\_OECD.M, and STEO.RGDPQ\_NONOECD.M pointing to STEO.PATC\_NON\_OECD.M. Hill Climbing then attaches the remaining macroeconomic variables (Alvi, secs. 3.4, 4.2.6). Alvi expects that, even if the re-learned network changes during validation, the basic expert framework will still exist (Alvi, sec. 3.4).

Finally, the problem is naturally discrete and regime-based. Alvi's trading logic is to be long before bull regimes and short before bear regimes (Alvi, sec. 3.3). Belief networks work with variables that have a fixed number of discrete states, while the raw EIA and FRED series are continuous (Alvi, sec. 3.2.2). Alvi bridges this gap with Hidden Markov Models. For each series, monthly changes are converted into up or down emissions, the Baum-Welch algorithm learns a three-state model, the Viterbi algorithm recovers the most likely regime sequence, and each state is labelled bull, bear or stagnant by its mean change (Alvi, secs. 3.3.1, 4.2). The discretized series then become the inputs to the Bayesian network.

## 3. Advantages of the Methodology

The first advantage is interpretability. A learned graph shows which physical market factors and macroeconomic indicators affect the oil market and how they are causally related (Alvi, abstract). Alvi argues this helps traders and risk managers understand market structure, and helps economists and energy policymakers decide which factors to target with policy, for example to avoid recessions or reduce dependence on fossil fuels (Alvi, Contributions to Science; ch. 5).

The second is probabilistic inference from evidence. Once learned, the network can be queried with known values to predict the state of an unobserved variable. Alvi plans to use the EIA's Short-Term Energy Outlook forecasts as evidence, so that the model returns a forecast of the oil price (Alvi, sec. 3.1.1). The predicted regime is used directly as a trading signal: Alvi's simple strategy goes short on a predicted bear regime, long on a bull regime, and takes no action on a stagnant one (Alvi, sec. 4.3.1).

The third is efficient and controlled estimation. Because the joint distribution factorizes over the graph, parameter learning splits into a set of lower-dimensional problems, each solvable with standard estimators such as maximum likelihood or Bayesian estimation (Alvi, sec. 2.1.1.1.1). Sparser graphs need fewer parameters, which gives a computational incentive to keep the model simple. Scoring functions such as the Bayesian Information Criterion trade fit against model complexity, which reduces overfitting (Alvi, sec. 2.1.1.1.2.2). Alvi also notes that a Bayesian model's performance may increase only until a certain number of datasets are incorporated, after which it may begin to overfit (Alvi, sec. 1.1).

The fourth is the combination of expert knowledge and data. The expert seed graph anchors the model in economic reasoning, while structure learning removes the need for an expert to relate every dataset by hand (Alvi, sec. 3.4). Alvi presents the result as an almost autonomous system that needs no prior expert knowledge beyond the choice of datasets, and that can learn and improve its trading decisions over time (Alvi, ch. 5).

## 4. Limitations Evident in the Thesis

The thesis also shows where the approach falls short, which frames the later stages of this project. On the validation set, the model's error rate in predicting the WTI regime was 67.86% (Alvi, sec. 4.3). With three possible states, a predictor guessing uniformly at random would be wrong about 67% of the time, so, by our own calculation rather than one Alvi reports, the model performs no better than chance on that set. Alvi himself concludes that further research is needed before concluding with confidence whether the model outperforms the EIA's Short-Term Energy Outlook under different stress events (Alvi, sec. 4.3.1).

The discretization step reduces each monthly change to a binary up or down emission (Alvi, sec. 4.2), which discards the size of each move. Two parts of the original motivation are also not carried through: the futures–spot regression proposed in section 1.1 does not appear in the implementation (Alvi, ch. 4), and while stress testing is set out as a task (Alvi, sec. 1.2.4), no stress-test results are reported. Finally, because the EIA and FRED data are monthly, Alvi notes that statistical confidence is only achievable for multi-year trends (Alvi, secs. 2.3.1–2.3.2).
